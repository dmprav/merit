# Concepts and language

These working definitions connect the journeys. Retain a funder's own terms when
describing a particular program.

## Funding structure

| Concept               | Meaning in this guide                                                            |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Funding program       | An organized approach to supporting a purpose through grants.                    |
| Funding opportunity   | A route to support, with a scope and application conditions.                     |
| Funding round         | A bounded intake and decision cycle; some opportunities operate without rounds.  |
| Portfolio             | Grants considered together for strategy, oversight, or learning.                 |
| Application           | A request and supporting material, which may develop through several stages.     |
| Application case      | A funder's record of receiving, preparing, assessing, and deciding a request.    |
| Applicant / recipient | The party seeking support / receiving an award and responsible under its terms.  |
| Project               | A defined body of work; grants can also support ongoing organizational activity. |

[Grants.gov](https://grants.gov/learn-grants/grant-terminology) provides US federal
terminology. Elsewhere, labels differ: “beneficiary” may mean a funded organization
in EU documents or people who benefit in evaluation discussions.

**General operating support** funds an organization's overall work; **project
support** funds a specified purpose. **Fiscal sponsorship** provides an
organizational home for work through another organization. **Matching funding**
depends on other contributions.
([Candid glossary](https://candid.org/resources/glossary-nonprofit-terms/))

A funding opportunity describes the route to support; a round organizes a bounded
cycle of work. A funder may also organize received applications for committee
review without operating their original application process.

An **application case** belongs to the funder's work. Its **recorded material** is
the version of the request and supporting information available for assessment.
Staff summaries, assessments, and recommendations interpret that material; they
are not part of what the applicant supplied. A **submission** is a particular
delivery of material by or on behalf of the applicant, distinct from a funder's
later recording or correction of it.

## People and attribution

A **contact** represents a person a workspace works with, independently of whether
they have an account. **Reviewer** describes that person's role in assessing an
application; it is not a separate kind of person. A **Merit account** identifies
someone who can sign in. **Workspace membership** establishes participation and
responsibilities in a foundation's workspace. A reviewer member can enter that
workspace, but only a **review assignment** grants access to the application
material they are asked to assess. Program managers can also receive assignments;
their separate staff access does not make another person's draft visible.

**My work** presents a person's assignments within the current workspace. It is a
view of work owned by the relevant capability, not a separate task lifecycle.

An applicant need not have an account in Merit. The **applicant contact** is the
person to approach about a request; the **recorder** is the person who entered
information on the applicant’s behalf. Neither being named as a contact nor
recording a request changes who is seeking funding. Permission to access an
application is separate from these facts.

Applicant and funder describe roles in a funding relationship. One organization
may seek funding and distribute funding in different relationships. Neither role
requires membership in the other party's organization.

A reviewer's judgment and the act of recording it also have separate authorship.
When a manager enters an assessment received elsewhere, the reviewer remains the
author of the judgment and the manager is its recorder. Merit's
[contact workflow](../workflows/manage-contacts.md) describes how shared contacts
support these relationships.

## Evidence and uncertainty

Source material can support a claim or judgment while leaving questions about its
accuracy, relevance, or completeness. Keep the following distinctions visible in
records, summaries, and explanations:

| Concept         | Meaning                                                                                                       |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Source material | Information supplied or observed, with its origin and relevant version.                                       |
| Claim           | A statement about the world that may be supported, disputed, or unverified.                                   |
| Verification    | Checking a specific claim against identified evidence or a method, with limits on what the check establishes. |
| Interpretation  | Meaning or implications drawn from source material, including assumptions and uncertainty.                    |
| Judgment        | An evaluative position, such as an assessment or recommendation, made by an identified person or body.        |

Traceability establishes where information came from and what material informed
an interpretation. Recording a statement preserves what was supplied; confidence
in its accuracy depends on the supporting evidence and any checks performed.
Attribute verification to the particular claim and check so it cannot imply that
an entire application or source is correct.

For example, an applicant's survey may support a claim about transport needs.
A reviewer can accept that evidence while disagreeing about whether transport
arrangements are sufficient to fund the work. Preserve the claim, its source,
and the reviewer's reasoning so another person can understand the disagreement.
Human and AI interpretations need the same distinction between source statements
and conclusions drawn from them.

Missing information stays distinct from a negative finding, an unstated position
from a deferral, and an unknown amount from zero. A newer source version identifies
changed material; its relevance to an earlier judgment requires consideration.
Keep conflicting judgments and unresolved questions inspectable when summarizing
or recording a subsequent recommendation. The
[review workflow](../workflows/review-applications.md) applies these principles to
received assessments and their bases.

## Judgments and commitments

**Eligibility** determines whether a request can be considered. **Assessment**
judges it against criteria. **Due diligence** examines the proposed recipient and
the risks of proceeding.

A **review request** records work requested from a reviewer for an application.
For work completed in Merit, a **review assignment** connects that request to the
invited account and its current workspace membership, with explicit acceptance,
conflict handling, and access revocation.
An **assessment** records the judgment received; it may fulfil a request or arrive
without one. Its **review basis** is the application material and criteria assessed,
which may differ from what is current when the assessment is recorded. Receiving
an assessment, knowing its basis, and having all its conclusions stated are
separate facts. In Merit, **review freshness** compares that basis with current
material and criteria; it does not establish the judgment's quality or require a
new review. Criteria revision identity and criteria content equality remain
separate comparisons under the [review basis policy](../workflows/review-applications.md#review-basis-and-freshness-policy).

A **recommendation** proposes an action; a **decision** records the authorized
choice. At [Wellcome](https://wellcome.org/research-funding/guidance/how-wellcome-makes-funding-decisions/how-wellcome-assesses-funding-applications),
committee rankings inform staff decisions alongside budget and strategy.

An **award** establishes support under stated terms. A **grant agreement** records
the parties' commitments; an **amendment** changes them.
([European Commission guidance](https://commission.europa.eu/funding-and-tenders/managing-your-project/managing-your-project-under-grant-agreement_en))

A **budget** needs an owner and scope: the funder's available resources differ
from the recipient's planned costs. Requested, recommended, awarded, paid, and
spent amounts represent different stages of the funding relationship.

**Closeout** settles final administrative and financial requirements. It can finish
while longer-term results remain uncertain. See [delivery and closure](journeys/deliver-and-manage.md).

## Authority and approval

**Permission** determines whether a person or client may perform an operation.
**Delegation** lets a client act for a person within granted access and the person's
current authority. **Approval** is an explicit acceptance of a particular proposal
or consequential action by someone with the required authority. Authorship,
permission, and approval answer different questions; recording an action does
not establish that its content was written or individually reviewed by that person.

A suggestion proposes a possible change. Accepting it can become a request to
save that change; a requested edit can also be carried out directly under delegated
authority. The owning workflow defines any additional approval requirement from
the action's consequences. Web and MCP must preserve that requirement. See
[authority across interfaces](../foundations/architecture.md#authority-across-interfaces)
for Merit's enforcement and attribution boundaries.

## Evidence and results

| Concept    | Meaning                                                                   |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Activity   | Work undertaken, such as running a workshop.                              |
| Output     | Something produced or delivered by the work.                              |
| Outcome    | A change associated with outputs, such as changed behavior or capability. |
| Impact     | Broader or longer-term effects, including unintended effects.             |
| Monitoring | Ongoing information about progress and performance.                       |
| Evaluation | Systematic examination of the work, its value, or its results.            |

These distinctions follow the [OECD evaluation glossary](https://doi.org/10.1787/632da462-en-fr-es).
For a workshop, sessions delivered are outputs; changed participant behavior concerns
outcomes. Delivering an activity alone establishes neither the resulting change
nor its cause.
